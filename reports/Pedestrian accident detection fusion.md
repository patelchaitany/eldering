# Teaching a crash app to protect pedestrians

An Android crash-detection app can add pedestrian protection with the sensors it already uses. The detection logic, though, has to come from fall-detection research, because **no published study has detected a pedestrian being struck by a vehicle from phone or wearable sensors, and no public dataset contains such an impact**. The closest prior art is a Sony patent and the large, mature literature on smartphone fall detection. That literature answers the multi-sensor fusion question clearly. The design that works is a cascade: a cheap accelerometer trigger, then a learned classifier over accelerometer and gyroscope windows, then confirming signals combined by voting (barometric height drop, orientation change, post-event stillness). Hand-tuned multi-sensor rules and "every sensor must agree" logic both lose to this design. The field's weak point is reality: the best-documented real-world smartphone deployment saw sensitivity fall from about 95% in the lab to **73%**, with only **37.5% precision** on real falls. A countdown the user can cancel is therefore unavoidable. For the "trip to the past" feature there is open ground. No crash or SOS product sends responders a timestamped last-hour trail with landmarks; they send a point or a live link. Apple Check In's "route traveled" is the nearest analogue, and it goes only to a chosen contact. Android can keep that trail cheaply in a 65-minute encrypted on-device ring buffer fed by a Fused Location Provider whose rate depends on the user's activity. Landmarks should be resolved only at incident time. The trail is delivered as an expiring web link when data is available, with an SMS fallback carrying coordinates and a compact encoded trail.

## Nobody has measured a pedestrian strike on a phone

The literature search turned up a clear gap. Phone-sensor research on pedestrian safety works *before* a collision: detecting distracted walking from the IMU (GaitX reports **92.3% accuracy** across 21 subjects, phone handheld or pocketed) ([PMC12390545](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12390545/)), fusing phone GPS and IMU with a Kalman filter for vehicle-to-pedestrian warnings ([Electronics 2024](https://doi.org/10.3390/electronics13020331)), or warning when UWB time-to-collision falls below 3 s ([PMC10142004](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10142004/)). Smartphone "accident detection" papers such as WreckWatch and iBump concern vehicle occupants ([WreckWatch](http://www.dre.vanderbilt.edu/~jules/wreckwatchj.pdf); [IJACSA survey](https://thesai.org/Downloads/Volume9No9/Paper_52-A_Survey_on_Smartphone_based_Accident.pdf)). The only explicit pedestrian design found is **Sony's patent US10803721B2** (granted October 2020). It names "a person ... hit by a car or falls" as a use case and describes a staged design. An accelerometer threshold starts vital-sign measurement, help is escalated in grades according to how far the vital signs deviate from the user's baseline, and the alert is suppressed if gait and vitals quickly return to normal. It reports no accuracy figures ([Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/US10803721B2/en)). This research covered web-indexed sources and could not run full IEEE Xplore or Scholar queries, so a small conference paper may exist. No validated sensitivity or false-alarm figure for vehicle-strike detection surfaced anywhere.

Consumer products cover pedestrians only by accident. Apple Crash Detection is scoped to passenger-vehicle occupants and does not mention pedestrians or cyclists ([Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/en-us/104959)). Pixel Car Crash Detection requires in-car activity recognition and does not trigger on bikes ([Google Store](https://store.google.com/magazine/new-pixel-safety-features?hl=en-US)). Garmin's Incident Detection runs during recorded outdoor walk, run and bike activities, notifies only personal contacts, and is labelled supplemental ([Garmin manual](https://www8.garmin.com/manuals/webhelp/GUID-5D183A14-BB43-4A9B-B441-5F824214CE40/EN-US/GUID-CE8456FF-55FB-4BF4-BCAB-9871D9EB5D18.html)). The documented pedestrian and cyclist rescues come from Apple Watch *fall* detection firing after a vehicle strike, as when an unconscious San Francisco cyclist hit by a car had 911 called for him ([iDrop News](https://www.idropnews.com/news/apple-watch-fall-detection-sends-paramedics-in-just-minutes-after-cyclist-gets-hit-by-car/126765/)). That points to the practical conclusion: **a good fall detector already catches many vehicle strikes, because a strike ends in a fall.**

Biomechanics says what extra signal a strike adds. Post-mortem human subject (PMHS) tests at 40 km/h show a two-impact event. The bumper strikes the leg at t=0, the head hits the hood or windscreen **135–141 ms later** after the body wraps onto the vehicle ([Forman et al., IRCOBI 2015](https://www.ircobi.org/wordpress/downloads/irc15/pdf_files/49.pdf)), and an NHTSA full-scale test measured about 125 ms, with the highest accelerations in that window ([NHTSA](https://www-nrd.nhtsa.dot.gov/pdf/BIO/Proceedings/2004_32/32-11.pdf)). A secondary ground impact follows, with highly variable kinematics (wrap, forward projection, vault, somersault) ([Accid. Anal. Prev. 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0001457522002287)). The 50% risk of a moderate (AIS2+) head injury occurs at an impact speed of about **39 km/h** ([Chalmers](https://research.chalmers.se/en/publication/162986)), so many survivable urban strikes still leave the victim unable to call for help. From these findings the expected phone signature, which is an inference not yet tested on any phone, has four parts: a sharp mostly horizontal acceleration, more than 90° of trunk rotation within about 150 ms, an airborne or sliding phase lasting from hundreds of milliseconds to seconds, then a second ground impact and often stillness. A trip-and-fall has one, mostly vertical impact. A dropped phone shows near-zero-g free fall and one spike with no gait beforehand. A GPS displacement of several metres (the throw distance) is a further hint that an ordinary fall never produces. Two caveats limit how much this signature can be trusted. Published peak torso and pelvis g-values could not be retrieved, and no study records what a *pocketed phone* sees, since a phone in a pocket or bag can decouple from the body and register its own impacts. Earlier crash-test work also showed that budget Android accelerometers saturate at **2, 4 or 16 g per axis** ([Paciorek et al., ICCS 2021](https://www.iccs-meeting.org/archive/iccs2021/papers/127470294.pdf)), so a strike will often clip. Features should therefore measure duration at full scale, velocity change and multi-axis energy rather than peak g.

## Fusion research is mature for falls, and it favours learned cascades over sensor stacking

Multi-sensor fusion has been studied extensively, but almost entirely for falls; the closest relatives are cycling and car crashes. The literature uses three levels of fusion. *Data-level* fusion combines raw signals, for example a complementary filter merging accelerometer and gyroscope into orientation. *Feature-level* fusion concatenates per-sensor features before classifying. *Decision-level* fusion combines per-sensor verdicts, and Bayesian, Dempster–Shafer and fuzzy rules are all discussed as ways to do it ([Tsinganos & Skodras 2018](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/18/2/592)). The most useful head-to-head result is counter-intuitive. On MobiAct, a **single-sensor accelerometer state machine followed by a kNN classifier reached F1 0.929**. Published accelerometer-plus-gyroscope threshold-fusion algorithms scored 0.581, 0.908 and 0.628, and the authors concluded that "a machine learning strategy should be preferred" over complex multi-parameter thresholds ([Tsinganos & Skodras, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5856082/)). A multi-position study found that requiring *every* sensor to vote "fall" (an AND rule) **reduced sensitivity** once more than two positions were combined. The best combination managed only about 0.6 sensitivity at 95% specificity, and hopping produced 46–55% false fall labels ([Casilari et al. 2016, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0168069)). Late (decision-level) fusion is favoured for robustness because one failing sensor cannot poison the result. A waist-IMU-plus-camera late-fusion system cut the false-positive rate to **3.6%**, against 11.3% for the IMU alone ([PMC12526565](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12526565/)). Learned early fusion with attention wins when there is enough data, reaching 94.2% accuracy versus 91.0% without attention, though that result is for fall-risk prediction rather than detection ([PMC13527089](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13527089/)). No smartphone benchmark compares all three levels, or Kalman and Dempster–Shafer methods, on one dataset with real-world false-alarm rates. **The practical lesson is to fuse at the decision level, with a learned core and voting confirmers, not to pile on thresholds.**

Each sensor has a defined job. The accelerometer is the trigger. The gyroscope, or Android's fused rotation-vector sensor, measures orientation change. The **barometer** detects height loss, including slow falls that accelerometer integration misses, but it must be used in short relative windows. In the P2Est study, indoor "altitude" drifted from 209.27 m to 191.64 m over a single day with the weather, and three phones side by side disagreed by up to 8.5 m. Calibrating the phone-to-floor pressure offset while the user walks gave decimetre accuracy. The same study found that walking downstairs caused false positives, and that lying on a 0.43 m couch looked like a fall for a chest-pocket phone ([Luo et al. 2022, P2Est](https://doi.org/10.1145/3478027)). Air pressure changes by roughly 12 Pa per metre of height near sea level (ρg ≈ 1.2 × 9.8), so a pocketed phone falling 0.3–0.8 m registers only about 4–10 Pa. That works over a few seconds and is lost in weather drift over minutes. **Post-event inactivity** is the main false-positive filter. One 2024 Android system alerts only if a second stage confirms the user cannot move ([arXiv 2412.09980](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.09980)). The **microphone** is weak evidence: an accelerometer-plus-audio app reached 96% sensitivity but only 77% specificity ([ISC record](https://search.isc.ac/Inventory/8/947527.htm)). Android also forbids starting a microphone foreground service from the background ([FGS restrictions](https://developer.android.com/develop/background-work/services/fgs/restrictions-bg-start)), so audio is unrealistic for always-on walking mode. Proximity and light sensors are useful for gating, for example putting the model to sleep when the phone is out of a pocket, as the open-source BeSafeBox app does ([GitHub](https://github.com/Foxpace/BeSafeBox_Android_app)). No study quantifies how much adding a barometer or microphone to accelerometer and gyroscope actually reduces false positives on a phone, which is a real evidence gap.

Three facts about these datasets matter for a commercial product. MobiAct, the best phone-in-pocket set, is licensed for non-commercial use only. FallAllD is the only public set with a barometer. **None contains phone audio or GPS during a fall, and none contains a vehicle impact**; the cycling equivalents, such as Kaggle's handlebar-phone set, have few labelled crashes ([Kaggle](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/shashwatwork/cyclist-accident-prevention-dataset)). Cross-dataset testing makes performance drop "dramatically" ([Igual et al. 2015](https://zaguan.unizar.es/record/48442)), and real falls combined with long daily-life recordings degrade sensitivity, specificity and hourly false-alarm rates ([Measurement 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0263224124008777)). Training data for a pedestrian-strike model must therefore be built from proxies: fall datasets resampled and rotated to mimic a pocket, synthetic IMU traces from human-body-model crash simulations, which can already predict head-impact timing with R² 0.979 ([Springer review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11831-023-09949-2)), staged dummy impacts with a phone attached, and the app's own countdown labels.

The real-world gap sets the product's tolerances. Northwestern's Galaxy S5 system, which used a 2 g trigger followed by elastic-net regression on 40 accelerometer and gyroscope features, scored AUROC 0.995 in the lab. Over **2,070 user-days** with 23 high-fall-risk participants it caught **27 of 37 real falls**, with precision of 37.5% and about one false alarm per 46 days. Six of its ten misses never crossed the 2 g trigger, 42% of its false alarms were stumbles, and battery life averaged only **12.2 hours** ([JNER 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8353784/)). A personalised-threshold Android system still produced more than one false positive a day ([Casilari et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.3390/s150817827)). At consumer scale, iPhone Crash Detection triggered by skiing generated 134 false emergency calls, over 14% of call volume, in one Japanese region in five weeks ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crash_Detection)). The trigger threshold, not the classifier, is the sensitivity bottleneck. A lower trigger only works if the second stage is strong.

## No paper unifies walking, cycling and driving, but the parts exist

No peer-reviewed framework was found that first classifies the travel mode on a phone and then dispatches to separate fall, bike-crash and car-crash detectors. Commercial products use this pattern implicitly, and every component is published. Transport-mode classifiers separate still, walk, run, cycle, car, bus and train with macro-F1 around **86–88%** (T2Trans) ([PMC9459749](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9459749/)), and up to 95% accuracy from accelerometer, gyroscope and magnetometer alone ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2590198224001672)). Hierarchical designs put a human-versus-vehicle split at the top ([arXiv 2005.07539](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.07539)). Mode-specific detectors are well developed for cycling. A probabilistic IMU classifier tested with crash dummies reported **96.8% sensitivity and 99.6% specificity** ([Accid. Anal. Prev. 2021](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0001457521003420)). A Chalmers algorithm that requires acceleration, rotation *and* speed criteria to all be met caught all six naturalistic crashes with zero false alarms, though on a tiny sample ([Chalmers](https://publications.lib.chalmers.se/records/fulltext/211381/local_211381.pdf)). A 2026 smartphone study showed that an ML second stage cut threshold false positives by **87–98%** for abrupt swerves ([Sensors 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/s26165138)). Field data shows why the second stage matters: in a 119-rider naturalistic e-scooter and e-bike study, **91% of "strong jolt" flags were road infrastructure** ([TRID](https://trid.trb.org/View/2743022)).

Two findings change how "walking mode" should be built. First, falls do not happen mostly while walking. In Northwestern's real-world data, Google Activity Recognition labelled the minute before a fall as tilting 41% of the time, still 26%, on foot only 11% and bicycle 4% ([PMC8353784](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8353784/)). A detector that runs only during `WALKING` would miss most real falls, which matters especially for older users. **The fall and impact detector must stay armed whenever the phone is on the body; activity context should tune thresholds and location rate, not switch detection on and off.** Second, an accident often *causes* a mode change, such as vehicle to still or walking to still. If the detector re-gates immediately, it discards the context it needs. The pre-event mode should be held for 30–60 s. Hard speed gates like WreckWatch's 15 mph, which cannot see low-speed crashes ([WreckWatch](http://www.dre.vanderbilt.edu/~jules/wreckwatchj.pdf)), have no place in pedestrian or cycling modes.

Android supports this design. The Activity Recognition Transition API reports `WALKING`, `RUNNING`, `ON_BICYCLE`, `IN_VEHICLE` and `STILL` enter and exit events, with latency that varies by device ([Android Developers](https://developer.android.com/develop/sensors-and-location/location/transitions)). Receiving a transition is an explicit exemption that allows starting a foreground service from the background ([FGS restrictions](https://developer.android.com/develop/background-work/services/fgs/restrictions-bg-start)). A `location`-type service started this way gets location only if the app holds `ACCESS_BACKGROUND_LOCATION` ([Android 14 FGS types](https://developer.android.com/about/versions/14/changes/fgs-types-required)), and the continuous trail needs that permission anyway. One Play policy constraint ties the two features together: **an app may declare only one background-location feature** ([Play Console Help](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9799150)). Detection and the trail should therefore be presented as a single feature, along the lines of "detect accidents and show responders where you were", rather than two.

## No safety product hands responders the last hour

Products that keep location history and products that raise alerts are separate today. Google moved Maps Timeline onto the device in 2024–25, closed it to third parties and, during the move, lost some users' data ([TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/computing/software/google-maps-is-about-to-get-a-big-privacy-boost-but-fans-of-timeline-may-lose-their-data); [GSMArena](https://m.gsmarena.com/google_has_inadvertently_deleted_some_peoples_maps_timelines-news-67095.php)). Life360 sells history by tier, 2 days on Silver and 30 on Gold, but its crash detection is vehicle-only above 40 km/h and gives responders coordinates, not a trail ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/life360-family-safety-gps/id384830320); [AAP](https://www.aap.com.au/?p=179550)). Pixel and Garmin send a live-tracking link ([Google Store](https://store.google.com/magazine/new-pixel-safety-features?hl=en-US); [Garmin](https://www8.garmin.com/manuals/webhelp/GUID-607F08F6-33FC-40BF-9727-84E54043D82D/EN-US/GUID-64F677A5-03E0-453F-BFE3-E1B93D6FA3FC.html)). Noonlight and RapidSOS pass dispatchers a single point plus profile data ([GovTech](https://www.govtech.com/biz/Noonlight-Brings-Caller-Information-to-911-Dispatch-for-Free.html); [RapidSOS](https://rapidsos.com/enterprise/oem-connected-vehicles/)). The one product that ships *past* movement automatically is **Apple Check In at the "Full" level**, which sends a contact the "route traveled" and the location of the last iPhone unlock when the user fails to respond ([Apple Personal Safety Guide](https://support.apple.com/guide/personal-safety/ips56b5bc469/web)). A last-hour trail annotated with landmarks is therefore a real differentiator. It is most valuable exactly where a single coordinate fails: a pedestrian struck in a dense street, or an older person found disoriented after a fall indoors, where GPS error and multiple floors make "you are here" ambiguous.

Building the trail cheaply is a solved engineering problem with one stale number. Android's guidance is to batch non-foreground location with `setMaxUpdateDelayMillis`, so the processor wakes once per batch while each fix keeps its own timestamp, and to listen passively to fixes other apps request, which "incurs no battery drain" ([Android Developers](https://developer.android.com/develop/sensors-and-location/location/battery/optimize)). `BALANCED_POWER_ACCURACY` "very rarely uses GPS" ([Android Developers](https://developer.android.com/guide/topics/location/battery)). The only published per-hour figures are from 2013: about **7.25% battery per hour** at high accuracy with 5 s fixes, against **0.6% per hour** at balanced accuracy with 20 s fixes ([PhoneArena](https://www.phonearena.com/news/Googles-new-location-APIs-the-biggest-change-to-location-since-Android-launched_id43153)). Modern chips are more efficient, so these figures bound the design rather than predict it. Indoors and in urban canyons, step-based pedestrian dead reckoning (PDR) fills the gaps. Fusing accelerometer, barometer and Wi-Fi has reached **99.1% real-time floor accuracy** in a seven-storey building ([PMC8271586](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8271586)). PDR heading drifts, though, and phone barometers lag height changes by seconds, by different amounts on different phones ([PMC6832213](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6832213/); [SM2439](https://sensors.myu-group.co.jp/sm_pdf/SM2439.pdf)). PDR points should be shown as low-confidence.

Landmarks should be looked up only at incident time. Google reverse geocoding costs $5 per 1,000 calls after 10,000 free a month. Places Nearby Search costs **$32 per 1,000** after 5,000 free. Street View Static costs $7 per 1,000 ([Google Maps Platform pricing](https://developers.google.com/maps/billing-and-pricing/pricing)). About 10–15 lookups per incident (the accident point, dwell points and major turns) keeps 1,000 incidents a month inside the free geocoding tier. Looking up every point continuously across thousands of users would mean millions of calls. The public OpenStreetMap services cannot serve production traffic. Nominatim caps an application at one request per second summed across all its users and forbids systematic or periodic queries ([OSMF policy](https://operations.osmfoundation.org/policies/nominatim/)), and Overpass suggests about 100 queries a day for regular use ([OSM Wiki](https://wiki.openstreetmap.org/wiki/Overpass_API)). An offline city-level OSM POI extract stored on the phone (transit stops, named buildings, shops, road names) works with no network at accident time and costs nothing per lookup, subject to ODbL attribution. It is the right primary source, with Google Places as an online enhancement.

Sending the trail runs into Android's emergency limits, which were established in the earlier crash-detection research. An app cannot auto-dial emergency numbers, because `ACTION_CALL` is blocked for them ([Intent reference](https://developer.android.com/reference/android/content/Intent#ACTION_CALL)). It can auto-text personal contacts only under Play's "Physical safety/emergency alerts" `SEND_SMS` exception ([Play Console Help](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/10208820?hl=en)). A trail in SMS needs compact encoding. Google's encoded-polyline format stores only differences from the previous point, at 1e-5° (about 1.1 m) precision ([Google](https://developers.google.com/maps/documentation/utilities/polylinealgorithm)). Downsampled to around 100 walking points, it comes to roughly 500 characters, about four SMS segments. Its alphabet, however, includes a backtick that GSM-7 lacks, and a single backtick forces the whole message into UCS-2 at 70 characters per segment. The trail must be re-encoded into a GSM-safe alphabet before sending. On privacy, Play allows background location for "physical safety", but requires a prominent disclosure before the permission prompt, a demo video of about 30 s and a privacy policy covering location ([Play Console Help](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9799150)). India's DPDP Rules apply notice, consent and security duties from about **May 2027** and prohibit "tracking or monitoring" children ([DSK Legal](https://dsklegal.com/?p=26455); [Seclore](https://www.seclore.com/fundamentals/dpdp-rules-2025-compliance-guide/)). The industry is moving the same way: Google moved Timeline on-device and Apple's Check In data is reported to be end-to-end encrypted ([Gadget Hacks](https://ios.gadgethacks.com/how-to/use-your-iphones-new-check-in-feature-let-contacts-know-when-you-arrive-safely-your-destination-0385415/)). A rolling on-device hour that leaves the phone only during an incident is both the strongest privacy posture and the easiest to explain in a Play declaration.

## Design recommendations: one armed detector, three modes, one frozen hour

### The sensor pipeline stays armed and changes behaviour by mode

The core recommendation is a single foreground service of type `location`, running whenever protection is on. The activity mode changes the thresholds and the sampling cost, never whether detection runs. Accelerometer and gyroscope run continuously at 50 Hz with hardware batching. Evidence suggests 20–50 Hz is enough for fall classification, and Android's delivery jitter requires timestamp-based resampling either way ([UniMiB](https://arxiv.org/html/1611.07688v5)). Driving mode raises the rate toward the 200 Hz cap and keeps the earlier report's gated crash pipeline. The decision logic is the same four-stage cascade in every mode: a trigger, a learned classifier over a 4–5 s window, confirmers combined by k-of-n voting (never AND), and a shared countdown and escalation path.

| Stage | Walking / standing / still (default) | Running / cycling | Driving (existing pipeline) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Context gate | Transition API `WALKING`/`STILL` + step detector; proximity "in pocket" check | `RUNNING`/`ON_BICYCLE` + GPS speed 8–35 km/h | `IN_VEHICLE` or car Bluetooth + speed >15–25 km/h |
| Trigger | Signal-magnitude peak ~1.6–1.8 g (below the 2 g that caused Northwestern's misses) **or** free fall then impact; time-at-full-scale for clipping phones | Higher peak (~3 g) plus rotation-rate spike, to absorb potholes | Multi-axis energy and Δv over 50–100 ms; clipping-aware |
| Classifier | Fall model (kNN/gradient-boosted trees first, small 1D-CNN later) on acc+gyro features; separate **strike score** for multi-peak, horizontal Δv, >90° rotation within ~150 ms, second impact | Bike-crash model: acceleration + rotation + speed criteria (Chalmers-style) | Tree model → CNN as labels accrue |
| Confirmers (k-of-n) | Barometer drop 4–10 Pa (median 1–2 s before vs 2–5 s after, vetoed if stepping on stairs); orientation now horizontal; no steps and low motion for 10–30 s; GPS jump of several metres (strike only) | Speed falls to ~0 and stays there; orientation change; no pedalling cadence | Speed falls to ~0; barometer cabin spike; sound level only if mic FGS was started by the user |
| Suppression | Normal gait resumes within ~10 s (Sony-style); known phone-toss pattern (no gait before, isolated spike) | Bike lifted or carried (no speed before) | Continuing at speed after spike |
| Hold after event | Keep pre-event mode for 60 s | 60 s | 60 s |

Severity grading follows the Sony patent's escalation idea. A plain fall followed by quick recovery gets a soft "Are you OK?" prompt. A high strike score, or a fall followed by sustained stillness, gets the full alarm and countdown. The strike classifier should launch in shadow mode: it scores events and records labels, but does not change escalation until field data validates it, because no ground-truth strike data exists yet. Training starts from MobiAct-style pocket data (licensing permitting), UMAFall, UniMiB and FallAllD for barometer features, with SisFall and KFall rotated into pocket orientation. Every countdown resolution ("I'm OK", "I fell", "I was hit", "phone dropped") becomes a label, as Google already does for car crashes. Targets should be stated per event and per day, not per window: at most about one false alarm per user per month and at least 70–80% sensitivity on real falls, which is roughly what the Northwestern field result implies is attainable.

### The one-hour trail lives in a 65-minute encrypted ring buffer

| Element | Specification |
|---|---|
| Collection | Fused Location Provider. **Still:** passive only plus one dwell point. **Walking:** BALANCED, 15–30 s, min distance ~10 m, batch delay 2–5 min. **Vehicle, or after a near-trigger:** HIGH_ACCURACY at 5 s for a few minutes |
| Gap fill | PDR from `TYPE_STEP_DETECTOR` + rotation-vector heading when accuracy >30–50 m or no fix for 30 s; barometric floor delta; points tagged `source=pdr`, low confidence |
| Storage | Room/SQLite table `(ts, lat, lon, acc, alt, pressure, speed, bearing, source, activity, floor, wifi_hash)`; delete rows older than 65 min on insert (~400 rows max); SQLCipher or a Keystore-wrapped key; no cloud sync |
| Freeze | On trigger, copy the window into an `incident` table so it is never pruned; keep logging until the event resolves |
| Landmarks | At incident time only: offline OSM POI R-tree first (nearest named POI, transit stop, road, intersection); online Geocoding/Places for the accident point and up to ~10 key points (dwell ≥3 min, major turns, start of the hour); user-labelled places and known Wi-Fi names ("Home", "Office") |
| Narrative | Auto-generated, human-readable timeline: "14:05 left Home → 14:18 passed Rajiv Chowk Metro Gate 3 → 14:31 last movement on MG Road near Big Bazaar, 3rd floor uncertain ±15 m" |
| Display | Web map with accuracy circles, PDR segments dashed, dwell pins, Street View Static image of the last point and the last landmark, fetched server-side only when the link is opened |
| User control | View and clear the buffer, pause the feature, and see exactly what would be sent; Play prominent disclosure naming this single background-location feature |

### The sharing flow escalates within a minute and degrades gracefully

| Time | Action |
|---|---|
| T+0 | Trigger fires and the trail freezes. The app requests a fresh high-accuracy fix and resolves landmarks in the background so they are ready when the countdown ends |
| T+0 to T+30–60 s | Full-screen alarm, vibration and spoken prompt in the user's language. "I'm OK" / "Need help" / outcome label. Plain falls with gait recovery get the soft prompt |
| On "Need help" or timeout, data available | Upload the frozen incident window. Push and server-SMS to contacts with a link carrying a 128-bit random token (no coordinates in the URL), expiring in 24–72 h, revocable, viewer-logged |
| Same moment, no data | On-device multipart SMS under the Play safety exception. Line 1: "Possible accident, last location lat,lon ±12 m at 14:32 near [landmark]". Line 2: a maps URL. Line 3: `T1:` plus the GSM-safe encoded trail of about 60–120 points (Douglas–Peucker ~10 m), decodable by the contact's app or web page |
| Emergency services | `ACTION_DIAL` pre-filled with the local number (112 in India) on the user's screen and in the contact's message. Automatic responder contact only through a live-agent partner, never by robocall |
| Afterwards | Live sharing continues for the session. Server copy deleted 30 days after the incident closes (configurable). Labels feed the next model |

## Conclusion

The research reframes the problem. Pedestrian accident detection is not a new sensing challenge, since every useful sensor is already in the phone. It is a *data* and *arming* problem. The algorithmic answer is mature (a learned cascade with voting confirmers), yet field results collapse because thresholds miss low-g falls and because detectors are switched off at the wrong time, as the finding that only 11% of real falls start while walking shows. The app that wins will be the one that stays armed across modes, holds context through the moment of impact, and treats every countdown as a labelled training example. It will build the first real pedestrian-strike dataset as a side effect, an asset no academic group or OEM currently has.

The trail feature is the more defensible differentiator because it does not depend on detection accuracy. A late detection, a manual SOS, or a manual SOS after the detector missed the event all benefit from a frozen, landmark-annotated hour, and it answers the question a single coordinate cannot: *how did they get here, and which side of the road or which floor are they on?* Building it on-device with incident-only release also turns Play's one-background-location-feature rule and India's DPDP regime from obstacles into a coherent story: the app collects location continuously for one stated purpose, keeps only one hour, and lets it leave the phone only when someone may need to be found.
